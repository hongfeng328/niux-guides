Anti-Choking Suction Devices: A Parent's Guide

Read this first. A suction device is a second-line tool. If someone is choking: 1) Call 911 (or your local emergency number), 2) give back blows and abdominal thrusts (chest thrusts for infants), and only 3) use a device if those fail, following its instructions. No device replaces first aid.

What these devices are

Anti-choking suction devices are handheld, non-powered tools. A mask seals over the mouth and nose, and a plunger or bellows creates negative pressure intended to pull an obstruction out of the airway. They need no batteries or charging, which is why families keep them in kitchens, cars and diaper bags.

Counterfeits are a real problem

Unbranded copies of suction rescue devices have drawn FDA import alerts, and marketplace searches mix them in with genuine listings at half the price. A rescue device is the worst product category to save money on with a copy: check the seller name, the brand on the title, and the manufacturer's own store before buying.

Frequently asked questions

What is an anti-choking suction device?
A handheld, non-powered device that uses suction to try to clear an object from a choking person's airway. It is a second-line tool: calling 911, back blows and abdominal thrusts always come first.

When should one be used?
Only after calling 911 and after back blows and abdominal thrusts have failed, following the manufacturer's instructions.

Are these devices FDA authorized?
Suction choking-rescue devices sold in the US are FDA-registered medical devices. Registration is not FDA approval or clearance, and no device substitutes for first aid and emergency services.

Where should I keep it?
Where food is eaten — kitchen or dining room, visible and reachable in seconds, with everyone in the household shown where it is.
